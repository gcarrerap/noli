# Chapter 9: A Rainy Day and a Faraway Star

On Wednesday, it rained all day.

It rained on the pumpkins. It rained on the paper bats on the windows. It rained so much that the street looked like a little river.

There was no school that afternoon, because the teachers had a meeting. So Mari stayed home. Zib floated by the window, watching the rain.

Its antennae were still gray.

Mari tried to cheer it up. She showed Zib how to make a paper airplane. She showed Zib how to build a pillow fort. She showed Zib her favorite cartoon.

Zib said, "That is nice," to everything. But it said it in a small, quiet voice. And the antennae stayed gray.

At last, Mari sat down next to Zib on the window seat.

"Zib," she said, "are you sad?"

Zib was quiet for a long time. Rain went *tap, tap, tap* on the glass.

"Yes," said Zib. "On my planet, when it rains, my mama makes warm soup. My papa tells jokes. My little brother, Zub, jumps in the puddles and gets all wet." Zib's voice got smaller. "I miss them, Mari."

Mari felt her own eyes get a little wet. She thought about how she would feel if she were very far away from Mom and Dad and Abuela.

"I would miss them too," said Mari. "A lot."

---

Mari did not say, "Don't be sad." Abuela always said that sad feelings are like rain. You can't make them stop by yelling at them. You just wait with them, and keep each other company, until they pass.

So Mari just sat next to Zib. She put a blanket over both of them. Pepper came over and curled up on Mari's feet, which she almost never did when Zib was around.

"Your little brother is Zub?" said Mari after a while.

"Zub," said Zib. A tiny bit of pink came back to one antenna. "He is very small and very loud. He would like you."

"I'd like to meet him."

"Maybe someday," said Zib.

---

In the evening, the rain stopped. The clouds started to break apart.

Abuela put on her rebozo. "Come," she said. "I called Mrs. Crump. She says that after a big rain, the air is very clean. It is the best night to look at the stars."

So Mari, Abuela and Zib walked down Maple Street. The sidewalk was shiny and wet. The street lights made yellow puddles.

Mrs. Crump was waiting at her door with a lantern. "Come up, come up," she said. "The sky is waking up."

---

In the round room at the top of the house, the roof was open. The sky was black and clear, and full of stars.

Mrs. Crump turned the big brass telescope. She looked through it, and turned some knobs, and looked again.

"Zib," she said, "can you tell me what your sun looks like from here?"

Zib floated up to the window. It looked at the stars for a long time. Then it pointed.

"There," it said. "Those three stars in a line, like a belt. And then up, up, a little to the left. A small orange one."

Mrs. Crump moved the telescope. She looked. Then she smiled.

"Have a look," she said.

---

Zib put its big eye to the telescope.

And in the telescope, small and bright and orange, was a star.

"That is my sun," Zib whispered. "That is my home sun."

"I'm afraid we can't see your planet," said Mrs. Crump gently. "Planets around other stars are very small and very dim. Even the biggest telescopes on Earth can almost never see them. But your sun is right there. And your planet is right next to it, going around and around."

Zib did not move from the telescope for a long time.

Then, slowly, its antennae began to change. Gray, then light gray, then a soft blue. Then pink.

"My family is right there," said Zib. "Right there, next to that little orange light. Maybe my mama is looking at the sky right now too."

---

"I'm sure she is," said Abuela.

Mari hugged Zib, very carefully, because Zib was soft and round and hard to hug.

"We're going to get you home," said Mari. "I promise. Just like Leo promised on his shield."

"Thank you, Mari," said Zib.

On the walk home, the stars were bright over Maple Street. Zib floated between Mari and Abuela, and for the first time in two days, it hummed a little song.

---

Then, just as they got to their front gate, Zib stopped.

All three antennae shot straight up. They turned yellow. Then they turned yellow again, even brighter, two times, blink, blink.

"Two," said Zib. "I feel two seeds! They are not far away. They are right here, on our street. In two of the houses."

"Which houses?" asked Mari.

Zib's antennae wiggled and wobbled. "I cannot tell. They keep moving. Like somebody is carrying them from place to place."

Mari looked at all the houses on Maple Street, with their pumpkins and their ghosts and their big plastic spiders. Then she had an idea.

"Zib," she said, "in two more days, every house on this street will open its door. And every kid in town will knock on it."

"Why?" asked Zib.

Mari smiled. "Because it will be Halloween."
